Before we show you anything

Five checks. Every property. Whatever it is worth.

  1. Who actually owns it

    We pull the current Record of Rights from Bhu Bharati and confirm the name and extent match the person selling. Bhu Bharati replaced Dharani in April 2025 — if a seller shows you a Dharani printout, it is from the old system.

  2. Whether it can be registered at all

    We check the survey number against the Section 22A prohibited list. This is the check most people skip. A property can have clean ownership, a clean encumbrance certificate and clean mutation and still be impossible to register — because the restriction attaches to the survey number, not to the owner’s name.

  3. What is already charged against it

    We take an encumbrance certificate covering at least thirteen years and read it for prior sales, mortgages, court attachments and partitions. If a mortgage appears, we ask for the registered release deed. An unreleased charge survives your purchase.

  4. Whether the layout approval is real

    We verify the LP number on the authority’s own portal — never from a brochure or a photocopy — and confirm your specific plot sits inside the sanctioned plan rather than on open space or a road. Gram panchayat layouts inside HMDA limits have been barred from registration since 2020.

  5. What the zone allows

    We check the land use zone and whether the parcel falls inside the GO 111 catchment. If it does, you will know before you see the property — not after you have fallen in love with it.

The other half

Documents are only half of it.

In eleven years, the deals that fall apart mostly don’t fall apart over paperwork. They fall apart over price, and they fall apart over families.

A seller agrees, then changes his mind. Owners within one family can’t agree among themselves. Someone who wasn’t in the room turns out to have a say. It happens after the site visit, after the negotiation, sometimes after a token has changed hands — and no amount of record-checking would have caught it.

So before we take you out to see a property, we try to establish two more things.

Whether you can actually get there

This is the first thing buyers ask us, before anything about documents. Is there road access. What is the road’s real width on the ground against the sanctioned plan. Does it run across the layout or across somebody else’s land. Can a car reach it in the monsoon, not just in March.

Whether the seller is genuinely ready

Who actually owns it, how many of them there are, whether they agree, and whether the price they’ve quoted is one they will still accept in three weeks. We would rather find out now than after you have taken a day off work and driven out.

The thing nobody tells you

If the land is cheap near Gandipet, ask why.

GO 111 is a government order from 1996 protecting the catchment of the Osman Sagar and Himayat Sagar reservoirs. It covers 84 villages across six mandals — Moinabad, Chevella, Shankarpally, Shamshabad, Shabad and Rajendranagar — and restricts what can be built there.

That is most of the area we work in, and it is the single biggest reason land on this side of the city is priced the way it is.

Attempts to lift the order have been challenged in the Telangana High Court and remain unresolved. The restrictions still apply. Plenty of buyers have purchased inside the zone at a discount, betting it would be scrapped. That is a bet on policy, not the purchase of a clear, usable plot.

We tell you when a property is inside the zone, before you see it, and we price it on the rules as they stand today. If you want to take the long view, that is a decision you make with full information — not one we make for you by staying quiet.

Questions buyers ask.

What is GO 111 and does it affect land near Moinabad?

GO 111 is a 1996 order protecting the Osman Sagar and Himayat Sagar catchment. It covers 84 villages across six mandals, including Moinabad, Chevella, Shankarpally and Shamshabad, and restricts construction and development there. Repeal attempts have been challenged in the Telangana High Court and remain unresolved, so the restrictions still apply. Value the land on the rules as they stand today.

What is the Section 22A prohibited list?

It is a list of properties that Sub-Registrars are not permitted to register. Entries attach to the survey number rather than the owner’s name, which is why a plot can show clean ownership and a clean encumbrance certificate and still be impossible to register. It is the check most often skipped, and the one that stops a sale after money has already moved.

Dharani or Bhu Bharati — which record should I ask for?

Bhu Bharati replaced the Dharani portal on 14 April 2025. Ask for the current Bhu Bharati record. The window for correcting Dharani-era errors closed on 13 April 2026, so an error in the record is now a serious problem rather than a paperwork issue.

How do I check whether a layout is approved?

Ask for the LP number and verify it on the authority’s own portal, not from a brochure. HMDA layouts approved before April 2024 sit on the HMDA DPMS portal, later applications on BuildNow, and DTCP layouts on their own portal. Confirm your specific plot number is inside the sanctioned plan and not on open space or a road. Gram panchayat layouts inside HMDA limits have been barred from registration since 2020.
